  • What went to plan (Ticket 101, Actual): the Boss used the one-call check_customer_order_fulfillment overview, saw S = 0 and the invoice 501 block, and briefed Inventory first. But a human now has to rewrite the draft. That's the one output on the desk that wasn't usable as delivered.
2 · "It would have been simpler to do this with one agent and the tools." Agree or disagree?

Verdict: I mostly agree for this desk as it is today. I disagree that one agent would be the right long-term design for Campus Customs. The reasons below are split into "for one agent" and "for the team", each tied to the run.

Why one agent would have been simpler here

  1. The tools already do the joining. The Question 3 overview tools (check_customer_order_fulfillment, check_rent_due_and_cash, quote_bulk_order_discount) return each ticket's whole picture in one call. After its second step on every ticket, the Boss already had every fact the specialists later reported back (101, 102, 103: the Boss's own tool calls, Actual row 4).
  2. Most of the team's tool calls were re-reads. Across the three tickets the team made 37 MCP calls to create 4 records (requests #1 and #2, drafts #1 and #2): get_ticket ×11, get_cash_position ×8, check_rent_due_and_cash ×3 on 102, and quote_bulk_order_discount ×8 on 103. A single agent needed roughly 3–4 calls per ticket: the overview, cash, one write, one draft.
  3. The team caused the worst mistake. Draft #2's unbacked September 5 date happened because Customer Service was briefed before Accounting reported "no PO, cash is $160". One agent writing the draft would have had that fact in the same context, three lines up.
  4. The guardrails that actually protected the money are not in the team. They're in code: amounts read by the server, the duplicate-request guard (it absorbed the extra Accounting runs on 101 and 102), human-only approve_and_pay, and the in-transaction "never below $0" check (Cash tab). A single agent would get every one of them for free.
  5. Cost and time. 41 model requests, 151,195 tokens and about 10 minutes of agent time for three tickets. Even with gpt-5.6-luna, delegation multiplied the cost of a desk this size.

Why the team still earns its place

  1. Least privilege. In the team, only Accounting can call prepare_* and only Customer Service can call draft_customer_message (backend/config.py). One agent would hold every write tool, so a single confused turn could both queue a payment and promise it to a customer. The pay tool is human-only either way, but the blast radius is smaller with a team.
  2. A second opinion on money. On 103, the Boss only told Accounting to prepare a PO "only if appropriate", and Accounting's prompt (Ben Wyatt, "protect the essentials first") made the actual call to refuse the $264 PO. On 102, Facilities (Mr. Carson) checked the landlord's claim against the lease. Separate prompts with opposite instincts acted as checks a single prompt tends to blur.
  3. Focused prompts write better customer messages. Draft #1 followed every privacy rule in customer_service.md (no unpaid invoice, no costs, conditional date, real alternatives). A generalist prompt that also has to be a budget hawk and a lease clerk carries more rules for the model to juggle.
  4. Reviewable accountability. The board shows "What each agent did", and the audit trail records requested_by: accounting on both payments. When draft #2 went wrong, the trail shows exactly which agent, with which brief, at which depth. That's useful for a shop owner and for improving one prompt without touching the others.
  5. Growth. Three tickets is a toy load. With the new problems below (restocks, receipts, replies), a team can split the work, and the delegation caps already bound the cost.

What I'd actually build next: a sequential team, not a parallel one. A single "desk" agent (the Boss with Inventory's and Accounting's read tools) decides, and only then hands Customer Service a finished fact sheet: the decision, the price, whether a PO exists. Writes stay split by role. That keeps the least-privilege and accountability benefits, would have cut the re-reads, and would have prevented draft #2's date. The evidence is that 102, the one ticket where nobody was briefed before the facts existed, scored best.
